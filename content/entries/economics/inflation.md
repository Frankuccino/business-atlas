Inflation is the rate at which money buys less over time. It is usually discussed as a cost of living, but for a business it is a force acting on every price, wage, and contract.

The key insight is that inflation is a comparison, not a level. What matters is whether your prices and your costs move at the same speed. A business whose costs rise faster than its prices is shrinking without noticing.

## Worked example

A company signs a three-year contract at $10,000 a month. Inflation runs at 5% a year:

    Year 1   $10,000 nominal   = $10,000 real
    Year 2   $10,000 nominal   =  $9,524 real
    Year 3   $10,000 nominal   =  $9,070 real

The price never changed, but the customer is paying $930 less in real terms each year by year three — a 9.3% real price cut. Meanwhile the company's own costs, which rise with inflation, are up 10.2%. The margin compresses from both sides.

## Watch for

- **Fixed-price contracts are inflation exposure.** Build in an escalation clause, or the contract becomes a subsidy to the customer.
- **Wages lag inflation, then catch up.** Real wages fall first, which is pleasant for the employer, then resentment builds and the correction is sudden.
- **Inflation changes the hurdle for investment.** A project returning 6% looks good at 2% inflation and worthless at 7%.
