An interest rate is the price of money over time. It matters to a business in two ways: directly, as the cost of any borrowing, and indirectly, as the rate at which future cash is discounted to a present value.

The second effect is the one founders miss. A dollar of profit in five years is worth less than a dollar today, and the higher the rate, the less it is worth. Rising rates therefore punish long-horizon, capital-heavy businesses and favour ones that return cash quickly.

## Worked example

A project returns $1,000,000 in five years. Its present value at different rates:

    At 3%:   1,000,000 / 1.03^5 = $862,609
    At 7%:   1,000,000 / 1.07^5 = $712,986
    At 12%:  1,000,000 / 1.12^5 = $567,427

The same future cash is worth $295,182 less at 12% than at 3%. A company planning on a distant payoff is worth a third less purely because rates moved — with no change to the business.

## Watch for

- **Rates set the value of growth.** High rates make "grow now, profit later" expensive. That is why growth companies are hit hardest when rates rise.
- **Your runway is a rate-sensitive asset.** See [runway](#runway). Borrowing to extend it costs more when rates are high.
- **Cheap money creates bad habits.** Projects funded at 1% that would fail at 6% get built, and the failures surface only when rates normalise.
