The cash flow statement reconciles the gap between profit and cash. It explains why a company with $100,000 of profit can still have $20,000 less in the bank than it started with.

The three sections answer different questions. Operating cash flow asks whether the core business generates cash. Investing cash flow asks where that cash is being put to work. Financing cash flow asks who is funding the gap.

## Worked example

    Operating activities
      Net income                    100,000
      Depreciation                  + 20,000
      Increase in receivables       - 60,000
      Increase in payables          + 15,000
      Operating cash flow           = 75,000

    Investing activities
      Equipment purchase            - 40,000

    Financing activities
      Loan repayment                - 30,000
      Owner draw                    - 25,000

    Net change in cash             = -20,000

The company made $100,000 and ended with $20,000 less cash. The reason is in the receivables line: $60,000 of the profit has not been collected yet.

## Watch for

- **Positive operating cash flow is the goal.** A business that must keep raising money to fund operations is renting its survival.
- **Growth consumes cash.** More sales means more inventory and more receivables before the cash returns. Fast growth and a cash shortfall are the same event.
- **Depreciation is added back.** It reduced profit but not cash. Forgetting this is the most common way to misread the statement.
