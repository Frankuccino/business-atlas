Cost of goods sold is the cost of producing what you sold, and nothing else. It
includes materials, direct labour, and the overhead you can attribute to
production. It excludes rent for the office, sales salaries, and the software
your engineers use — those are operating expenses, and mixing them in is the
most common way to make a healthy business look broken.

The distinction matters because it decides what you compare. COGS against
revenue tells you the margin you earn by existing. Operating expenses against
gross margin tells you whether your business model scales or just your to-do
list.

## Worked example

A reseller reports:

- Revenue: $1,000,000
- Beginning inventory: $50,000
- Purchases during the year: $400,000
- Ending inventory: $80,000

    COGS = 50,000 + 400,000 - 80,000 = 370,000
    Gross margin = 1,000,000 - 370,000 = 630,000  (63%)

Now suppose next year revenue is flat at $1,000,000 but COGS rises to $500,000.
Gross margin falls to 50%. Revenue did not move; the business got worse. That is
the signal this term exists to give you, and it is invisible if you only read
revenue.

## Watch for

- **Capitalising what should be expensed.** Software and rent are not COGS.
  Putting them there inflates gross margin and hides a cash problem.
- **Inventory changes masking a price cut.** A falling ending inventory with
  flat purchases usually means you sold down stock, not that you got cheaper.
- **Comparing COGS across business models.** A manufacturer's COGS is a
  different animal from a SaaS company's, where COGS is hosting and support.
