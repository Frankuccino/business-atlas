Gross margin is the money you keep from each sale after paying to make the thing
you sold. It is the ceiling on everything else: if gross margin is 40%, then
sales, marketing, engineering, and rent all have to fit inside 40% of revenue,
and the remainder is profit or loss.

Two things make it worth learning properly. First, it is the number investors
ask for before they ask for growth — a business with 90% gross margin can lose
money for years and still be a good business; one with 15% cannot. Second, it
is the number pricing decisions show up in first, before volume or CAC.

## Worked example

Same reseller as [cogs](#cogs):

- Revenue: $1,000,000
- COGS: $370,000

    Gross margin = (1,000,000 - 370,000) / 1,000,000 = 63%

If the company spends $400,000 on operating expenses, operating profit is
$230,000, or 23% of revenue. If COGS rises to $500,000 with revenue flat,
gross margin falls to 50% and operating profit falls to $100,000 — a 57% drop
in profit from a 35% rise in COGS. That asymmetry is why this ratio gets so
much attention.

## Watch for

- **Gross margin is not profit.** It ignores every operating expense. Companies
  routinely celebrate 70% gross margin while losing money overall.
- **Contribution margin is the stricter sibling.** Gross margin still carries
  fixed production overhead; contribution margin strips variable selling costs
  too. See [contribution-margin](#contribution-margin).
- **A one-off price cut shows up here, not in volume.** If margin drops while
  revenue rises, you bought revenue.
