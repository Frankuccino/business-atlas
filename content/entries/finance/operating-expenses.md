Operating expenses — opex — are the costs of keeping the lights on: salaries for people who do not build the product, rent, software, marketing, legal, and the sales team's overhead. They are the costs that remain even if you sell nothing.

The distinction from COGS is the distinction between *variable* and *fixed* cost. COGS goes up when you sell more; opex mostly does not. That is why opex is where leverage lives: a company whose opex is flat while revenue doubles sees its margin expand without changing the product at all.

## Worked example

A SaaS company:

- Revenue: $1,000,000
- COGS (hosting, support): $150,000
- Operating expenses: $700,000 — engineering $350,000, sales and marketing $250,000, general and administrative $100,000

    Gross margin = 1,000,000 - 150,000 = 850,000  (85%)
    Operating profit = 850,000 - 700,000 = 150,000  (15%)

Now revenue doubles to $2,000,000 with COGS rising to $300,000 and opex rising only to $850,000:

    Gross margin = 2,000,000 - 300,000 = 1,700,000  (85%)
    Operating profit = 1,700,000 - 850,000 = 850,000  (42.5%)

Revenue doubled, profit grew 5.7x. That asymmetry is operating leverage, and it is the reason investors care about the opex line.

## Watch for

- **Opex is a choice, not a fact.** Hiring, office space, and tooling are decisions. Treating them as fixed is how costs creep.
- **See [break-even](#break-even)** — opex is the fixed cost you must cover before any sale becomes profit.
- **Capitalised costs hide here.** Software development is often capitalised rather than expensed, which flatters this line and moves the cost to the balance sheet.
