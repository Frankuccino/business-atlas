Revenue is the gross inflow from sales before any costs are deducted. It is the first line of the income statement and the denominator of almost every ratio in this atlas.

Three things make it treacherous. It is not profit — a company can grow revenue while losing money on every sale. It is not cash — revenue is recognised when earned, which may be weeks before the money arrives. And it can be bought — spending on discounts or acquisition can raise revenue while destroying margin.

## Worked example

A company sells 1,000 units a month at $50 each:

    Revenue = 50 x 1,000 = 50,000

Against that: COGS of $20,000 and operating expenses of $25,000.

    Gross margin = 50,000 - 20,000 = 30,000  (60%)
    Operating profit = 30,000 - 25,000 = 5,000  (10%)

Revenue doubled would be $100,000 on paper — but if the extra 1,000 units were sold at a $10 discount and cost $30 each to fulfil, the new units bring in $40,000 of revenue and $10,000 of gross margin. Revenue looks like growth; margin per unit fell from 60% to 25%.

## Watch for

- **Recognised revenue is not cash.** A $120,000 annual contract booked in January may pay monthly. Revenue and cash flow are separate statements.
- **Gross vs net.** Marketplaces and agencies report very different revenue for the same transaction depending on whether they report the full amount or only their fee.
- **See [runway](#runway)** — revenue is the input to whether you survive long enough to be right.
