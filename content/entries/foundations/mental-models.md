A mental model is a simplified picture of how a domain works — supply and demand, incentives, [opportunity cost](#opportunity-cost), [second-order thinking](#second-order-thinking). They are useful because the world is too complex to reason about directly, and a model compresses it.

The danger is forgetting that the map is not the territory. A model that explains most situations will mislead in the one that matters, and the confidence it produces is the warning sign.

## Worked example

Deciding whether to raise prices:

    Model: demand curve        — higher price, lower volume. Ask: how elastic is this market?
    Model: anchoring           — the new price becomes the reference for all future comparisons.
    Model: incentives          — what does the change reward the sales team to do?
    Model: second-order        — existing customers see the increase; what does that do to trust?

No single model answers the question. Four models together surface the trade-offs that one alone would hide. That is the actual value of a model collection: not answers, but a checklist of angles.

## Watch for

- **A model is a prompt, not a conclusion.** The right output of a model is a question, not an answer.
- **Using one model is worse than using none.** A single lens feels like clarity and is usually just a blind spot.
- **Models expire.** A model that explained a market at one scale may not explain it at another.
