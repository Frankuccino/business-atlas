Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you did not choose. It is not a line on a balance sheet — it never appears in the accounts — which is exactly why it is so often ignored.

A decision is only rational when the chosen option beats the alternative, not when it beats nothing. Building a feature for three months is profitable if the alternative was nothing; it is a loss if the alternative was a feature that would have brought in twice the revenue.

## Worked example

You have one engineer for a quarter. Two options:

- **Option A** — rebuild the checkout: costs $60,000 in salary, expected to lift conversion by 2%, worth $200,000 a year.
- **Option B** — fix the onboarding funnel: costs $60,000, expected to lift activation by 10%, worth $120,000 a year.

Option A looks better on its own. But choosing A means the $120,000 from B is the opportunity cost. The comparison is:

    A net:  200,000 - 120,000 = 80,000
    B net:  120,000 - 200,000 = -80,000

A still wins, but by half what it looked like. If B had been worth $250,000, A would have been the wrong call despite the bigger headline number.

## Watch for

- **Sunk costs are not opportunity costs.** Money already spent is gone and should not weigh on the decision. Only the forward-looking alternatives matter.
- **Time is the hidden one.** Two options costing the same cash can have very different opportunity costs if one takes three times as long.
- **See [pricing](#pricing)** — the same discipline applies to price: a discount's real cost is the full-price sale you gave up.
