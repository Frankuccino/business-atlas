Second-order thinking is the discipline of following a decision past its immediate effect. The immediate effect is usually obvious and often good; the next one is where the cost hides.

It is not about being cautious. It is about seeing the whole chain before committing, so that a decision that looks free is recognised as expensive.

## Worked example

A company cuts its support team from 10 to 6 to save $240,000 a year:

    First order:   payroll down $240,000. Obvious, measurable, immediate.
    Second order:  response time rises from 10 minutes to 45.
    Third order:   satisfaction falls; churn rises 2 points; at $200 ARR per customer and 5,000 customers, that is $200,000 a year lost.
    Net:           the saving cost $200,000 and bought a worse product.

The first-order view shows a $240,000 win. The full chain shows a $40,000 loss and a product that is harder to sell. Both are true; only one was examined.

## Watch for

- **Time is the mechanism.** Second-order effects arrive later, so they are never present at the moment of decision. You have to go looking for them.
- **Ask "and then what?" three times.** Most chains are short enough to trace, and the third question usually surfaces the real cost.
- **Not every second-order effect is negative.** Some decisions look expensive and pay back twice. The discipline works in both directions.
