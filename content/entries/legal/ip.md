Intellectual property is the law's way of protecting things you can copy but cannot touch. The main types are patents for inventions, trademarks for names and marks, copyright for written and creative work, and trade secrets for information kept confidential.

The practical point for a young company is that IP is often lost through process, not through a competitor. An employee who invents something may own it, not the company, unless the contract says otherwise.

## Worked example

A software company with a distinctive name and a novel algorithm:

    Copyright      The source code is protected automatically on creation. Registration helps enforce it.
    Trademark      The name must be registered to be defended reliably; an unregistered mark is hard to stop others using.
    Patent         The algorithm may be patentable, but software patents are slow, expensive, and jurisdiction-dependent.
    Trade secret   The customer list and pricing are protected only if access is controlled and contracts say so.

The most common failure: a contractor builds the product, and nothing in their contract assigns the IP to the company. The company then owns nothing it paid for, and the contractor can sell the same code to a competitor.

## Watch for

- **IP must be assigned in writing.** A contractor, co-founder, or early employee who creates something owns it by default in many jurisdictions unless a contract says otherwise.
- **Registration is not optional for trademarks.** A name you use is not a name you own.
- **IP is a moat, not a product.** Owning a patent does not make customers want the product. See [moat](#moat).
