BATNA is what you do if the negotiation fails. It is the standard against which any offer should be measured, and it is the true source of negotiating power.

The common mistake is negotiating against your hopes rather than against your alternative. A customer offering $80,000 feels acceptable if you hoped for $70,000 — but if your alternative is doing nothing, $80,000 is the best outcome available and the customer knows it.

## Worked example

A company is choosing a supplier. Its BATNA is an in-house build costing $120,000 and three months.

    Supplier A offers $90,000        →  better than BATNA by $30,000. Acceptable.
    Supplier B offers $130,000       →  worse than BATNA by $10,000. Build in-house.
    Supplier C offers $85,000 but is unreliable  →  the risk may make the true cost exceed $120,000.

The BATNA is not a number to mention — it is a decision rule. Knowing it means the company can walk away from B without regret, which is exactly why it can afford to.

## Watch for

- **Improve your BATNA before negotiating.** A second quote, an in-house option, or a delay all strengthen your position more than any tactic at the table.
- **Do not reveal it.** Telling the other party your alternative hands them the ceiling of your flexibility.
- **Estimate theirs.** A supplier with no other customers has a weak BATNA and will concede more. Power is relative, not absolute.
