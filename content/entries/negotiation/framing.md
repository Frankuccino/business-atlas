Framing is the effect of context on a decision. The same price described as a discount from a higher number feels like a gain; described as a premium over a lower one, it feels like a loss.

It matters commercially because most negotiations are not about the number but about the reference point the number is measured against. Whoever sets that reference point usually wins the argument.

## Worked example

A freelancer quoting $4,000 for a project:

    Frame A:  "The project is $4,000."                    — no reference. The client compares it to their budget.
    Frame B:  "A full rebuild is $9,000. This is $4,000." — the client compares it to $9,000 and sees a saving.
    Frame C:  "This is $4,000, or $3,200 if we start this month." — the client compares two options and picks.

The work and the price are identical in all three. B anchors a higher number; C reframes the choice as a decision between two gains rather than a cost. Neither is dishonest, and both change the outcome.

## Watch for

- **Someone is always framing you.** A supplier's "our standard rate" is a frame. Ask what the alternatives are before accepting the reference point.
- **Framing is not lying.** Omitting a relevant comparison is not deception; it is presentation. Know the difference and stay on the right side of it.
- **The strongest frame is the true one.** A frame that collapses under one question costs you the relationship.
