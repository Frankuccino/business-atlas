Delegation is giving someone else a result to own, with the authority to decide how to achieve it. The two parts are inseparable, and most delegation fails on the second.

The reason it is hard is that the founder's judgment is the business's advantage, and handing it over feels like giving that up. But judgment applied to one task is a bottleneck, and the goal is to multiply it by building judgment in other people.

## Worked example

A founder handles every customer refund personally. Delegating well:

    Task:    "Approve refunds up to $200."
    Bad:     "Let me know about any refund request and I'll decide."
    Good:    "Refund up to $200 without asking me. Above $200, bring me the case. Review the pattern monthly with me."

The bad version delegates the work and keeps the decision — so the founder is still the constraint, and now also reviews every request. The good version delegates the decision within a boundary, keeps the founder informed at a level they can act on, and creates a person who learns to judge.

## Watch for

- **Delegation needs a boundary.** Authority without limits is abdication, and the failure mode is a decision the founder would never have made.
- **You cannot delegate what you cannot define.** If you cannot state the outcome, the other person cannot own it.
- **Delegation is an investment with a return lag.** The first attempt usually takes longer than doing it yourself. That is the cost of building capacity, not evidence the approach failed.
