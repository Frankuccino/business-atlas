Jobs to be Done says customers do not buy products; they hire them to make progress. The classic example is a drill: nobody wants a drill, they want a hole, and the hole is a means to hanging a shelf.

The practical payoff is a competitor set that includes everything else the customer might do. The rival to a project-management tool is not another tool — it is the spreadsheet, the email thread, and the decision to do nothing.

## Worked example

A customer buys a bookkeeping app. The surface answer is "they want accounting software." The job is different:

    Functional:  know whether the business made money this month
    Emotional:   stop feeling anxious about the numbers
    Social:      look credible to an accountant or investor
    Circumstance: it is the first month they have been too busy to keep up

The competitor set is now spreadsheets, an accountant, a bookkeeping service, and ignoring it. Each competes on different terms. An app that wins on "stop feeling anxious" — by showing a single plain-English number on day one — beats one with more features but a steeper learning curve.

## Watch for

- **The job is not the feature.** "They asked for a dashboard" is a solution, not a job. Ask what progress they wanted from the dashboard.
- **Circumstance is the unit.** The same person has different jobs in different situations. Segment by circumstance, not by demographic.
- **Doing nothing is a competitor.** Most products lose to inaction, not to a rival. If the customer's current state is tolerable, you have no job to win.
