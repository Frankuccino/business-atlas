An MVP is not a smaller version of the final product. It is an experiment: the minimum build that can test the riskiest assumption behind the business.

The distinction matters because most "MVPs" are actually incomplete products. A product missing its core value cannot test demand — it can only test whether people tolerate missing features.

## Worked example

A company believes small firms will pay $50 a month for automated invoicing. Two approaches:

    Approach A — build the full product: recurring billing, integrations, reporting, mobile app. Six months. Then launch and find out.
    Approach B — build only invoice generation, deliver it manually behind the scenes, charge from day one. Two weeks.

Approach B tests the real assumption: will someone pay? If 10 customers pay, the company knows to build Approach A. If none do, it spent two weeks instead of six months. The manual work is not a shortcut — it is the point, because it buys certainty.

## Watch for

- **The assumption must be named.** "Test whether people want this" is untestable. "Test whether a 20-person firm pays $50 for automated reminders" is.
- **An MVP can be a conversation.** A landing page, a priced offer, or a manual service can all be MVPs. Shipping code is the most expensive option.
- **MVPs accumulate debt.** The shortcut is only valid if you plan to rebuild. Otherwise the experiment becomes the product.
