A network effect means the product's value is a function of how many people use it. Telephones, marketplaces, and payment networks all become more useful as they grow.

The consequence is a winner-take-most dynamic. Once a network passes a tipping point, customers are not choosing the better product — they are choosing the one their contacts already use, and no amount of feature work overcomes that.

## Worked example

A messaging app's value to a user is roughly the share of their contacts on it:

    20% of contacts on it   →  you check two apps. Slightly useful.
    60% of contacts on it   →  you move most conversations. Clearly useful.
    90% of contacts on it   →  leaving means losing touch with everyone.

The jump from 60% to 90% is where the moat forms. A competitor with better features at 5% share offers less value than the incumbent at 90%, because value came from the network, not the features. That is why network-effect markets usually produce one dominant player.

## Watch for

- **Not every user adds value.** A social network full of accounts that never post does not strengthen the network. Density within a community matters more than total users.
- **Network effects can be local.** A marketplace can be strong in one city and weak in another; growth must be concentrated, not spread.
- **They decay.** If the network stops being useful — spam, noise, irrelevance — the same mechanism accelerates the exit.
