Customer acquisition cost is the total cost of sales and marketing divided by
the number of customers that effort produced. It is the price of growth.

The trap is that it is almost always reported blended — all spend over all
customers — and a blended number can hide a broken channel inside a healthy
average. Two channels can each be individually bad and jointly look acceptable.
The useful form is per channel, per cohort, over time.

## Worked example

In one quarter a company spends:

- Paid ads: $120,000 → 400 customers
- Content and SEO: $60,000 → 100 customers
- Sales team (2 reps): $180,000 → 300 customers

    Blended CAC = (120,000 + 60,000 + 180,000) / 800 = $450

    Per channel:  ads $300   |   content $600   |   sales $600

The blended number suggests $450. The real picture: ads are the only channel
under $450, and content — the cheapest to scale — costs $600. If the customer
pays back $500, content and sales are losing money while ads barely break even.
That conclusion is invisible in the blended figure.

## Watch for

- **Attribution inflates CAC in your favour.** Last-click credit will hand the
  sale to the cheapest channel and make every expensive one look worse, or
  vice versa. Say which model you are using, because it changes the number.
- **CAC is not payback.** A customer acquired for $450 who pays back in three
  months is a different business from one who takes three years. Pair this with
  [contribution-margin](#contribution-margin) and payback period.
- **See [roas](#roas)** for the same trade-off expressed as revenue per dollar
  spent rather than cost per customer.
