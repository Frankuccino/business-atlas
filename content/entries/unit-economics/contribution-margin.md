Contribution margin is gross margin taken one step further. Gross margin
subtracts the cost of producing the thing; contribution margin also subtracts
everything else that changes when you make one more sale — payment processing,
sales commission, shipping, a salesperson's time, ad spend attributed to the
sale.

It is the number that decides whether an offer is worth making. Gross margin
can be healthy while contribution margin is negative, because the sale costs
more to acquire and fulfil than it brings in. That is a profitable-looking
company quietly paying customers to arrive.

## Worked example

An online course sells for $200. Per sale:

- Payment processing: $6
- Affiliate commission: $40
- Hosting and support: $10
- Ad spend attributed to the sale: $90

    Contribution margin = 200 - 6 - 40 - 10 - 90 = $54 per sale

Each sale puts $54 toward fixed costs. If the course has $10,000 in fixed
costs, you need 186 sales to break even, and every sale after that is $54 of
profit.

Now raise the ad spend to $160 per sale: contribution margin becomes -$16. You
are now losing $16 every time someone buys. Doubling traffic doubles the
losses. That is the trap this term exists to catch.

## Watch for

- **Ad spend belongs here, not in operating expenses.** If you exclude
  acquisition cost you get a number that looks like contribution margin but
  behaves like gross margin.
- **It is per unit, and units differ.** A $54 contribution on a $200 course is
  not the same as $54 on a $2,000 course. Compare the percentage too.
- **See [roas](#roas) for the ratio view** of the same trade-off: how much you
  spend to earn a dollar of revenue.
