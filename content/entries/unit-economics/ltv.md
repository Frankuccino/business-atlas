Lifetime value is the profit a customer produces from the day they arrive to the day they leave. It is the other half of the unit-economics equation: CAC tells you what a customer costs, LTV tells you what they are worth.

The two together decide whether growth is worth buying. A customer worth more than they cost to acquire makes growth cheap. A customer who costs more than they return makes growth a way to lose money faster.

## Worked example

A subscription costs $30 a month with a contribution margin of $21 after variable costs. The average customer stays 14 months:

    LTV = 21 x 14 = 294

If acquiring that customer costs $98, the ratio is 294 / 98 = 3.0x, and the customer pays back in about 4.7 months. Now suppose churn rises so the average stay falls to 7 months:

    LTV = 21 x 7 = 147
    LTV/CAC = 147 / 98 = 1.5x

The acquisition cost did not change. The business halved its value per customer, which is why churn and LTV are the same conversation.

## Watch for

- **Use contribution margin, not revenue.** Gross margin is a reasonable proxy; revenue is not, because it ignores the cost of serving the customer.
- **LTV is an average.** Averages hide the difference between customers who stay two months and customers who stay two years.
- **Cohort, not aggregate.** An LTV computed across all customers mixes old cohorts with new ones and hides whether newer customers are better or worse.
