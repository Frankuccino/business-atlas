Payback period is the time a customer takes to pay for their own acquisition. It is the bridge between unit economics and cash flow, and it is the number investors check most often after CAC and LTV.

The distinction it makes is between a cost that returns quickly and a cost that ties up capital. Two businesses can have identical CAC and LTV and completely different cash situations purely because of payback.

## Worked example

CAC is $300. The customer pays $50 a month with a contribution margin of $35:

    Payback = 300 / 35 = 8.6 months

Now the same customer on a $20 plan with a $14 contribution margin:

    Payback = 300 / 14 = 21.4 months

Same acquisition cost. The cheaper plan takes 21 months to pay for itself, which means the business must finance the customer for over a year and a half. That is why a low-price plan with the same CAC can be far worse than a high-price one.

## Watch for

- **Use contribution margin, not revenue.** Paying back on revenue flatters the number; the cost of serving the customer is real.
- **Payback sets your capital need.** Adding customers adds to the amount you must finance. Growth beyond your capital is a funding problem, not a sales problem.
- **Longer payback needs longer customers.** If payback is 12 months and the average customer stays 10, the customer never pays for itself. See [ltv](#ltv).
